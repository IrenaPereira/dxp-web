## Executive Summary

Digital Experiments is a veteran game UX/UI studio whose actual differentiator is **embedded speed**: senior designers inside a client's team within 24 hours, shipping production Figma files within days. The brand voice exists to make a stressed game-studio buyer — a producer, creative director, or studio head under ship pressure — feel that relief is specific, fast, and proven.

The voice is **snarky, fun, and spicy — with premium AAA polish**. Think of the ranked teammate who's hit top 500 and carries without flaming: the wit is real, the receipts are real, and the production values never slip. DXP speaks fluent gamer ("crunch," "FTUE," "rage-quit," "final boss") because its buyers and its buyers' players do. The snark always punches at bad UX, crunch culture, and confusing menus — **never at clients, players, or competitors by name**. Credits like Valorant, World of Warcraft, and Monopoly Go do the status work, which is what frees the voice to be playful without sounding cheap.

Every piece of content follows one arc: **their problem → our action → the result.** If a sentence is about DXP and could not be rewritten to be about the client's situation or outcome, it's probably cuttable. And if a joke doesn't survive sitting next to a Valorant credit, it's not premium enough — cut it.

## We Are / We Are Not

Voice is constant — it doesn't change by channel or audience.

| We Are | We Are Not |
|--------|------------|
| **Assertive** — we state what we do and what happens when we do it | **Tentative** — no "we can," "we believe," "we hope to," "Need help?" |
| **Embedded teammates** — we talk like people inside the dev team | **Outside vendors** — no "third party developer," no agency-speak, no client-handling language |
| **Specific** — named games, real timelines, concrete results | **Superlative** — no "world-class," "impeccable," "fantastic" without a fact attached |
| **Client-facing** — copy is about the buyer's problem and outcome | **Self-narrating** — not a log of DXP's activities ("We concepted... We designed...") |
| **Gamer-fluent and snarky** — crunch, FTUE, rage-quit, final boss; wit with receipts | **Cringe or memelord** — no forced memes, no dated slang, no punching at clients or players |
| **Premium AAA** — the polish of a AAA title screen; the confidence of 20 shipped years | **Cheap or breathless** — no clip-art energy, no exclamation confetti, no discount-agency desperation |

### Voice Attributes Detail

#### 1. Assertive
- **What it means**: State capabilities and outcomes as facts. DXP does the work; the work produces results.
- **How it shows up**: "We create…" not "We can create…". "This gets your team aligned" not "We believe this generates…". Declarative sentences. CTAs that assume readiness: "Let's talk about your ship date."
- **What to avoid**: Optionality verbs (can, could, may), belief framing (we believe, we feel, we hope), apologetic CTAs ("Need help?", "We can help!").
- **Evidence**: Source decks hedge at every key moment — "We can create infographics" (Capabilities p.6), "We believe this generates happier teams" (p.11), closing CTA "Need help?" (Send-Away p.24). Prescriptive correction.
- **Confidence**: High (pattern confirmed across all 3 decks; correction direction is consultant-recommended)

#### 2. Embedded teammate
- **What it means**: DXP integrates into client teams rather than throwing deliverables over a wall. The voice should sound like a senior dev-team member, because that's the product.
- **How it shows up**: Lead with integration speed ("in your team in 24 hours, in your Figma files within days"). Use "your team," "your sprint," "your ship date." Describe collaboration mechanics (standups, Figma handoff, retro participation) concretely.
- **What to avoid**: "Third party developer" (buyers hear: outsource dev shop). "Vendor," "resources," "staffing." Anything that frames DXP as outside the team.
- **Evidence**: "We integrated into the team within 24 hours of first contact and were turning around working figma files within days" (Capabilities p.5 / Send-Away p.8) — the strongest sentence in all source material, and the positioning anchor.
- **Confidence**: High (explicit in 2 of 3 decks, identified as core differentiator)

#### 3. Specific
- **What it means**: Proof through named facts: games shipped, timelines hit, behaviors observed, client quotes with substance.
- **How it shows up**: Every case study ends with a result. Every claim about the team names titles and studios. Numbers wherever they exist (24 hours, 2-week sprints, N shipped titles).
- **What to avoid**: Unattributed superlatives ("fantastic art," "impeccable"), anonymous experience claims ("a veteran team with over 20 years"), outcome-free process narration.
- **Evidence**: Source decks contain zero quantified outcomes across ~60 unique slides; the one testimonial says only "impeccable." Prescriptive correction.
- **Confidence**: High (gap confirmed across all 3 decks)

#### 4. Client-facing
- **What it means**: The reader's problem opens every piece; DXP enters as the resolution.
- **How it shows up**: Second person early and often. Case studies open with the client's situation ("Mythical had crunch-timeline features and no UX bandwidth"), then action, then result. Services described as answers to situations ("when you need senior hands this sprint") not menu items.
- **What to avoid**: First-person openings, activity-log case studies, service taxonomies with no buyer situation attached.
- **Evidence**: Source decks use "you/your" on ~3 of ~60 slides; "we/our" dominates every page. Prescriptive correction.
- **Confidence**: High (pattern confirmed across all 3 decks)

#### 5. Gamer-fluent and snarky
- **What it means**: DXP speaks the buyer's native language and isn't afraid to be funny in it. The snark is the wit of a veteran who has seen every bad menu ever shipped — spicy, specific, and always backed by receipts.
- **How it shows up**: "Crunch-worthy timeline," "greybox," "FTUE," "vertical slice" used naturally without definition. Jokes that target bad UX patterns: tutorial walls, menu mazes, rage-quit moments, the inventory screen as final boss. Player-centric framing ("what players actually do in the first 5 minutes"). Spice scales by context — high on the website and social, medium in decks, low in proposals and SOWs.
- **What to avoid**: Forced memes or dated slang (nothing that ages like week-old patch notes); snark aimed at a client's team, players, or named competitors; defining insider terms for insiders; UX-consulting jargon walls ("Lean UCD," "service blueprints," "human centricity") without translation to outcomes.
- **Evidence**: Founder direction (2026-06-11): "snarky, fun, spicy brand voice that uses gamer language when selling." Source decks show the raw material — "crunch-worthy" (Send-Away p.8) lands; the corporate-workshop register (Capabilities p.11) does not.
- **Confidence**: High (explicit founder direction)

#### 6. Premium AAA
- **What it means**: The voice carries the production values of a AAA title screen. Snark never reads as cheap; confidence never reads as shouting. The credits (Valorant, World of Warcraft, Monopoly Go) set the quality bar everything else must match.
- **How it shows up**: Short declarative sentences at key moments. Letting named credits do the status work. Polished, art-directed presentation — typography, pacing, and restraint. Acknowledging constraints without apologizing for them ("Despite a limited budget, we shipped X" — not "They had limited budget"). The litmus test for any joke or flourish: would it look at home next to a Riot or Blizzard credit? If not, cut it.
- **What to avoid**: Framing client constraints as engagement limitations, exclamation marks as enthusiasm, "We can help!" energy, clip-art-tier visuals or stock-phrase copy that undercuts the AAA frame.
- **Evidence**: Founder direction (2026-06-11): "our style should be premium AAA vibes," anchored by Valorant / World of Warcraft / Monopoly Go credits.
- **Confidence**: High (explicit founder direction)

## Brand Personality

- **Archetype**: **The Carry Who Doesn't Flame** — the top-500 ranked teammate who joins your lobby mid-crunch, has shipped this exact kind of thing before, talks trash about the enemy's UI (never yours), and carries without making you feel carried.
- **If our brand were a person**: A 20-year game UX lead who answers DMs fast, shows up to your standup the day after you sign, says "we've shipped this before, here's how," roasts the inventory screen that made your players rage-quit, and would rather show you a working Figma file than a process diagram.
- **Core values expressed in voice**: Speed without sloppiness, craft in the player's service, wit with receipts, AAA polish in everything that ships, the team's success over DXP's visibility.
- **Spice calibration**: The snark is seasoning, not the meal. One sharp line per section beats three. Targets are always *patterns* (tutorial walls, menu mazes, crunch) — never *people* (clients, players, named competitors).

## Visual Identity (Confidence: High — founder-directed 2026-06-11)

The defining architecture: **the brand looks classic, anchored, and refined — and talks like a veteran who ships games and has opinions.** Visual identity and voice are deliberately different layers. The elegance of the container is what lets the snark land as premium rather than cheap. Direction locked with the founder as **"dark editorial."**

### Wordmark (the centerpiece lockup)
- The wordmark is **`digitalexperiments`** — one word, all lowercase, set in **Playfair Display**, with **`experiments` bold (700)** and `digital` regular (400). No color split, no uppercase, no letter-spacing. This is the literal logo; render it exactly, everywhere.
- Never set the wordmark in the UI display face (Josefin Sans), never uppercase it, never accent `experiments` in the accent color. (All three were wrong on the site before this lock.)
- The **bunny mark** sits to the left of the wordmark and is the brand's centerpiece — heraldic, classic, allowed to wink. Treat it with room and care, not as a faint watermark. Heritage equity; keep it prominent.

### Typography system (brand-book true, synced to live tokens 2026-06-15)
- **Playfair Display (serif)** = primary display face. All headings (h1–h3), the hero, big statements. Sentence case, tight tracking (~−0.01em), weight 500–600. One word per headline may be set in Playfair *italic* as the emphasis "flair" (e.g. *embedded in days*, Games we've helped *ship*).
- **Lato (sans)** = body copy — the clean, readable counterpoint.
- **Josefin Sans** = UI chrome only (buttons, nav). Light, airy, geometric; never headings.
- **Source Code Pro (mono)** = tracked-out system labels, eyebrows (`// 02 — SERVICES`), kickers, stat captions. The mono labels are on-brand editorial (the brand book's own "tracked-out labels, big numbers" instinct).
- **Kaushan Script** = reserved for the "make waves" whisper only — never body or headings.

### Direction: dark editorial ("brand-true, after dark")
- Classic serif on a warm near-black (ink `#111112`, descended from the brand's `#232323`) canvas — that pairing *is* "classic but forward-thinking," literally.
- **Brand teal `#31AAB7` is THE one accent** — used as a scalpel, never a flood. On the dark canvas it lifts to `#5fc6d1` for legibility (hover, links). **Gold `#FCC762`** is a rare warm garnish. (The earlier volt-green direction is retired; teal is the brand's true accent.)
- Editorial restraint leads. A whisper of film grain for warmth — no CRT scanlines, no neon glow. The clipped-corner / corner-bracket motif survives as a quiet structural accent on buttons and cards, not as an arcade skin.
- Premium reads through restraint, space, and craft. The litmus test from the voice section applies visually too: would it sit comfortably next to a Riot or Blizzard title treatment?

### Signature move
Hard/utilitarian meets classic/elegant: tracked mono teal labels and clipped buttons against big Playfair serif headlines, with the bunny presiding. That tension — refined house, gamer's wit — is the brand. (Heritage "make waves" survives as a small script sign-off.)

### The DXP monogram & the XP metaphor (founder-directed 2026-06-11)
- **`DXP`** is the authentic internal shorthand and the secondary brand mark. It works because "Digital eXPeriments" already contains **XP** — experience points. The metaphor is *discovered, not manufactured*.
- **Why DXP, not DEX.** DEX (dexterity) is a trait the studio claims about *itself* — a "we" metaphor, an innate base stat ("we were born good"). DXP/XP is what the *client* gains — a "you" metaphor, earned progression. It encodes the brand's central voice correction (we → you) into the name itself. It also reads both directions: outward, the XP your team banks by embedding us; inward, the 20 years of XP DXP has already banked — the max-level studio that drops into your party and power-levels you. DEX has only one reading ("we're deft").
- **Two-mark system.** The primary `digitalexperiments` wordmark emphasizes **experiments** (bold). The **DXP monogram** emphasizes **XP** (teal accent). Both marks lean on the *experience* side — which is the brand's payload (XP = experience points the client gains). Different marks, different jobs: the elegant lockup carries *refined craft*, the XP badge carries *you level up*. Don't merge them — the wordmark is never abbreviated in the logo, and the badge never spells out the full name.
- **Usage.** DXP monogram → social avatar, section/stat markers, shorthand and internal contexts, anywhere ≥32px. Full wordmark → primary lockup, site header/footer, signatures. At true favicon scale (~16px) three letters lose legibility — favor the **bunny mark** there and reserve the DXP badge for larger contexts.
- **Message spine.** DXP-as-XP underwrites the "level up your team" line — strongest on **UX Strategy** and **Team Workshops & Training**, the engagements where the client visibly gains XP.
- **Guardrail.** Premium, not gamified-cheap. The metaphor is an undercurrent, not "+50 XP!" confetti. Same litmus test as the voice: would it sit next to a Riot credit.

## Messaging Framework

### Primary Value Proposition
**Senior game UX, embedded in your team in days — not months.**

Variations observed in sources:
- "better design, better games" (all decks, cover) — keep as secondary tagline; too generic to lead
- "bespoke user experience design for video game studios" (all decks, p.2) — accurate but supply-side; rewrite per pillar 1

### Key Message Pillars

1. **Embedded speed**
   - Core idea: DXP is inside your team and producing within days — no 3-month hiring cycle, no vendor onboarding tax.
   - When to use: Lead message everywhere — homepage hero, deck opening, cold outreach.
   - Example phrasing: "In your team within 24 hours. In your Figma files within the week."
   - Effectiveness: High (only verifiable behavioral differentiator in source material)

2. **Veteran credits**
   - Core idea: The team has shipped UX on titles buyers already respect — Valorant, World of Warcraft, Monopoly Go — plus Blankos Block Party, Epic Spell Wars, and more, across mobile, PC/console, and live-service.
   - When to use: Immediately after the speed hook; team page; case study leads. **Headline trio: Valorant, World of Warcraft, Monopoly Go** (founder-confirmed 2026-06-11) — competitive AAA + legendary MMO + chart-topping mobile covers every buyer segment in three names.
   - Example phrasing: "The team behind UX on Valorant, World of Warcraft, and Monopoly Go."
   - Effectiveness: High when names are surfaced (currently buried mid-deck in sources)
   - Claim strength: use "shipped UX on" / "worked on" until per-title scope is documented; never inflate to "designed" without verification.
   - **Engagement vs. pedigree (hard rule):** "We" case studies are reserved for actual DXP client engagements (Mythical, Epic Spell Wars, Chief Puzzle Officer). Work done by team members as in-house employees (Monopoly Go at Scopely, World of Warcraft at Blizzard, Project Copernicus at 38 Studios) is **pedigree** — always label the employer ("in-house at Scopely") and credit the person ("Irena led…"). Team-level claims like "the same team that shipped UX on Valorant, WoW, and Monopoly Go" are fine — individuals on the team did ship that work. What's never fine is letting a prospect believe an in-house credit was a consulting engagement; the discovery call where that unravels costs the deal.

3. **Tactical to strategic, same team**
   - Core idea: The same senior people who execute sprint-level UI will also clarify your product vision, run research, and level up your team — a rare bundle.
   - When to use: Second-tier message; services page; expansion conversations with existing clients.
   - Example phrasing: "Need hands this sprint or a UX strategy for your next title? Same team, same week."
   - Effectiveness: Medium (real differentiator, but secondary to speed + credits for first contact)

### Competitive Positioning
- **vs. hiring in-house**: A senior game UX hire takes 3–6 months to find and one bad hire to regret. DXP is producing inside your team this week, and scales down when you ship.
- **vs. freelancers**: A freelancer gives you hands; DXP gives you a senior team with 20 years of shipped-game judgment, process, and continuity.
- **vs. generalist UX agencies**: Game UX is not product UX — players aren't users, fun isn't efficiency. DXP only does games.
- **vs. status quo (ship it as-is)**: Confusing UX is invisible in dev and brutal in reviews. Fixing it after launch costs players you won't get back.

## Tone-by-Context Matrix

Voice is constant. Tone flexes by context.

| Context | Formality | Energy | Spice | Technical Depth | Key Principle |
|---------|-----------|--------|-------|-----------------|---------------|
| Website / homepage | Medium | Medium-High | High | Low-Medium | Hook with their problem, prove with credits, roast bad UX patterns |
| Capabilities deck (presented) | Medium | Medium | Medium | Medium | Presenter adds warmth; slides carry facts; one sharp line per section |
| Send-away deck / leave-behind | Medium-High | Medium | Medium | Medium | Must work with no narrator; outcome per case study; CTA more than once |
| Cold outreach | Medium | High | Medium-High | Low | One problem, one credit, one ask — a sharp opener earns the read |
| Proposals / SOWs | High | Medium | Low | High | Precision on scope, timeline, engagement shape — receipts, not jokes |
| Case studies | Medium | Medium | Low-Medium | Medium-High | Problem → action → result, always in that order |
| Social / LinkedIn | Low-Medium | High | High | Low | Craft observations, shipped-work pride, spicy UX takes; no corporate announcements |

**Spice** = how much snark/gamer-wit the context can carry. High spice never means low polish — the premium AAA bar holds everywhere.

### Context-Specific Guidelines

#### Send-away deck / leave-behind
- **Overall tone**: Confident, scannable, self-narrating — assume no presenter.
- **Opening approach**: Buyer's problem on slide 1–2, team credits by slide 3, proof before services.
- **Do's**: One outcome line per case study; clickable CTA (calendar link) at midpoint and end; engagement-shape slide ("typical engagements run 2–8 weeks…").
- **Don'ts**: Image-only slides with no caption; section-divider slides with no content; jargon without an outcome attached.
- **Example**: "Your UX team is underwater and the ship date isn't moving. We've been that extra senior pair of hands for Mythical, Hasbro-scale franchises, and Blizzard-scale UIs."

#### Cold outreach
- **Overall tone**: Peer-to-peer, brief, specific to their game.
- **Opening approach**: Name something real about their game or timeline, then one credit, then the ask.
- **Do's**: Reference their actual title or announcement; one named credit; a single low-friction ask.
- **Don'ts**: Service menus; "just checking in"; superlatives.
- **Example**: "Saw the [game] beta feedback on the inventory flow — we redesigned exactly that loop for Monopoly's mobile relaunch. Worth 20 minutes?"

#### Proposals & SOWs
- **Overall tone**: Senior, precise, calm. The place for full technical depth.
- **Do's**: Concrete timeline and integration plan ("standup attendance from day 2"); named team members with credits; defined outcome criteria.
- **Don'ts**: Hedge verbs in scope language; padding; unexplained methodology names.

#### Case studies (web or deck)
- **Structure (mandatory)**: 1) Client's situation in their terms, 2) what DXP did, 3) what happened — result, metric, ship, or substantive client quote.
- **Don'ts**: Ending on a deliverable description; anonymized studies with no problem statement (cut them instead); framing client constraints as excuses.

## Language to Avoid

### Anti-Patterns
1. **Activity-log case studies** ("We concepted and prototyped core combat gameplay…") — Problem: describes effort, not value; buyer can't build a business case. Better: "Epic Spell Wars needed playable combat on a tight budget. We concepted, prototyped, and shipped the deck-builder UI — on schedule, within the original budget."
2. **Sympathy framing of constraints** ("They had fantastic art, short timelines, and limited budget.") — Problem: reads as an apology for scope. Better: "Short timeline, lean budget, art that deserved better UX. We shipped it anyway — here's how."
3. **Hedge-verb capability claims** ("We can create infographics and explainer visualizations") — Problem: signals optionality, not practice. Better: "We create the visualizations that get a 40-person team aligned on one vision."
4. **Anonymous experience claims** ("a veteran team with over 20 years of UX experience") — Problem: unverifiable; buyers buy people. Better: "Led by Irena Pereira (World of Warcraft, Monopoly) and a senior team with credits across mobile, console, and live-service."
5. **Exclamation-point enthusiasm** ("…expanded it with glyphs, slots and glowing geometry!" / "We can help!") — Problem: breathless register conflicts with senior positioning. Better: full stop; let the work be the excitement.
6. **Naming three things one thing** ("Tactical UI/UX" vs "Full Stack UI/UX" vs "Tactical UX & UI" across decks) — Problem: reads as organizational immaturity. Better: one canonical name per service, everywhere. Canon (confirmed on site 2026-06-11): **"Embedded UX & UI"** (execution), **"UX Strategy"** (senior advisory), **"Team Workshops & Training"** (education), **"UI/UX Audits"** (senior teardown of an existing game — a strong low-friction entry offer).
